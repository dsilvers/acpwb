# IT change management: CAB, standard/normal/emergency changes, rollbacks.
Q: A change advisory board (CAB) exists to:
+ Review planned changes for risk, timing, and impact before they happen
- Approve changes after they cause outages
- Choose the team's lunch order
- Replace testing

Q: A "standard change" is:
+ A low-risk, pre-approved change that follows a documented procedure
- Any change made by a senior engineer
- A change made on a standard business day
- A change nobody wrote down

Q: An emergency change:
+ Fixes an urgent problem and still gets expedited approval and a post-review
- Skips documentation forever
- Is any change requested by an executive
- Happens only during fire drills

Q: Every normal change request should include:
+ A description, risk assessment, test plan, schedule, and rollback plan
- A motivational quote
- Only the requester's name
- A promise that nothing will go wrong

Q: A rollback plan describes:
+ How to undo the change if it fails
- How to roll the server rack to a new room
- How to roll out the change faster
- How to blame another team

Q: Making a production change during a change freeze without approval is:
+ A policy violation, even if the change works
- Allowed if nobody notices
- Encouraged near quarter end
- Fine if you are confident

Q: After a change is implemented, the owner should:
+ Verify that it worked and close the change record
- Leave immediately for the weekend
- Delete the change record
- Make another unplanned change

Q: Separation of duties in change management means:
+ The person who approves a change is not the person who wrote and deployed it alone
- Engineers sit in separate rooms
- Changes are split into tiny pieces
- No one talks to the CAB

Q: A change that caused an outage should be followed by:
+ A blameless post-incident review to learn and prevent a repeat
- Quietly removing it from the change log
- Finding someone to blame
- A change freeze on all coffee machines

Q: Configuration changes to firewalls are:
+ Changes that require the change process like any other
- Exempt because they are quick
- Only reviewed annually
- Made by whoever has the password

Q: Change records are useful during audits because they show:
+ Who approved what, when, and why
- Which engineers type fastest
- The weather during each change
- Nothing; auditors ignore them
