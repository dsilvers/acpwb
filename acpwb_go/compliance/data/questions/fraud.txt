# Occupational fraud awareness.
Q: The "fraud triangle" describes three conditions that often lead to fraud:
+ Pressure, opportunity, and rationalization
- Profit, power, and popularity
- Planning, payment, and promotion
- Policies, procedures, and paperwork

Q: A common red flag of employee fraud is:
+ Living beyond one's means or refusing to take vacation
- Taking regular vacations
- Asking questions in training
- Arriving on time

Q: If you suspect fraud, you should:
+ Report it to the Ethics Hotline, Internal Audit, or Legal
- Investigate on your own and confront the person
- Ignore it unless you are sure
- Tell coworkers first

Q: The most common way occupational fraud is detected is:
+ Tips from employees, customers, or vendors
- Luck
- The weather
- Annual parties

Q: Segregation of duties prevents fraud by:
+ Making sure no single person controls a transaction from start to finish
- Separating the office into zones
- Splitting large purchases into small ones
- Giving everyone the same access

Q: Creating a fake vendor to receive payments is an example of:
+ Billing fraud
- Supplier diversity
- A cost-saving measure
- Vendor onboarding

Q: Expense reports for meals that never happened are:
+ Expense reimbursement fraud
- Estimates
- Allowed if small
- A form of tip

Q: Changing financial records to meet a quarterly target is:
+ Financial statement fraud and prohibited
- Earnings management best practice
- Required by the board
- Fine if reversed next quarter

Q: Employees who report suspected fraud in good faith are:
+ Protected from retaliation
- Required to prove it first
- Responsible for the investigation
- Paid a commission

Q: A vendor asks you to approve an invoice for work you know was not done. You should:
+ Refuse and report it
- Approve it if the amount is small
- Approve it and dispute it later
- Split the invoice

Q: Keeping evidence such as documents or emails related to suspected fraud is:
+ Important; do not delete or alter anything
- Unnecessary
- The fraudster's job
- Prohibited
