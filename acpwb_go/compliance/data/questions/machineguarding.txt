# Machine guarding. Answers follow OSHA 1910.212 and 1910.147 basics.
Q: Machine guards are designed to:
+ Prevent contact with hazardous moving parts
- Make machines look nicer
- Keep the machine warm
- Slow down production

Q: Removing or bypassing a guard to speed up work is:
+ Prohibited
- Allowed for experienced operators
- Fine during busy periods
- Required for maintenance without lockout

Q: The "point of operation" is:
+ The area where the machine cuts, shapes, or presses material
- The machine's power button
- The supervisor's desk
- The break room

Q: Servicing a machine where unexpected startup could cause injury requires:
+ Lockout/tagout
- Turning off the light
- Asking a coworker to watch the switch
- Unplugging the coffee maker

Q: Jewelry, loose clothing, and long hair near rotating machinery:
+ Can be caught and pull you in; secure or remove them
- Are fine if they are expensive
- Improve grip
- Are required for visibility

Q: Nip points are found where:
+ Two parts move together and can catch a body part, such as rollers or belts and pulleys
- Employees take naps
- Coffee is served
- Cables are stored

Q: A guard is missing on a machine you are assigned to use. You should:
+ Not use the machine and report it
- Use it carefully
- Make your own guard from cardboard
- Use it faster to finish sooner

Q: Reaching around or through a guard while the machine is running is:
+ Prohibited
- Fine with gloves
- Allowed for jams
- Allowed for supervisors

Q: Emergency stop buttons should be:
+ Easy to reach and tested to make sure they work
- Covered to prevent accidental use
- Removed from the machine
- Painted to match the wall

Q: Guards themselves must not:
+ Create new hazards, such as sharp edges or pinch points
- Be bolted on
- Be visible
- Be inspected

Q: Training on a machine should be completed:
+ Before operating it
- After the first injury
- Only by supervisors
- During the first week whenever convenient
