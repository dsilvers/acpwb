# Wire transfer fraud and business email compromise.
Q: Business email compromise (BEC) often involves:
+ A fake or hacked email asking to send money or change payment details
- A broken email server
- Too many emails
- A spam filter

Q: A vendor emails new bank details for future payments. You should:
+ Verify by calling a phone number already on file, not one in the email
- Update the details immediately
- Reply to the email to confirm
- Call the number in the email signature

Q: An urgent, confidential request from the "CEO" to wire money today should be:
+ Verified independently through known channels before acting
- Paid immediately
- Kept secret as asked
- Paid in gift cards

Q: Wire transfers above approval thresholds require:
+ Dual approval
- One approver
- No approval if urgent
- The requester's own approval

Q: A sign of BEC is:
+ A sender address that is slightly different from the real one
- Correct spelling
- A request you expected
- A normal invoice

Q: If you think you sent a fraudulent wire, you should:
+ Report it immediately to Treasury and Security so the bank can try to recall it
- Wait to see if it was real
- Send another wire to fix it
- Hide it

Q: Changing payroll direct deposit details based only on an email request:
+ Is not allowed without verification
- Is fine if it looks real
- Is required
- Speeds up payroll

Q: Fraudsters often time requests for:
+ Busy periods, holidays, or when executives are traveling
- Calm weekdays
- When auditors are present
- Never

Q: Attachments with "updated wiring instructions" should be:
+ Treated with suspicion and verified
- Opened and followed
- Forwarded to everyone
- Printed

Q: Callback verification means:
+ Calling a known, trusted number to confirm a request
- Calling the number in the suspicious email
- Calling back the next day
- Texting back

Q: Reporting a suspicious payment request that turns out to be real is:
+ Always the right thing to do
- Embarrassing and discouraged
- Grounds for discipline
- A waste of time
