# Hearing conservation. Answers follow OSHA 1910.95.
Q: OSHA requires a hearing conservation program when noise exposure equals or exceeds:
+ An 8-hour time-weighted average of 85 dBA
- 50 dBA
- 120 dBA
- Any noise at all

Q: OSHA's permissible exposure limit for noise is:
+ 90 dBA as an 8-hour time-weighted average
- 70 dBA
- 140 dBA
- 100 dBA for 12 hours

+ Cuts the allowed exposure time in half
- Doubles the allowed exposure time
- Has no effect
- Requires a day off

Q: Employees in a hearing conservation program must receive:
+ A baseline audiogram and annual audiograms
- Just one test at retirement
- No testing
- A test only after an injury

Q: Hearing protectors for employees in the program must be:
+ Provided at no cost, with a choice of suitable types
- Purchased by employees
- Shared between workers
- Optional decorations

Q: Training for employees in the hearing conservation program must be repeated:
+ Annually
- Every 10 years
- Never
- Only at hire

Q: A standard threshold shift is:
+ An average change of 10 dB or more at 2000, 3000, and 4000 Hz in either ear, compared to baseline
- A change in work shift
- A volume knob setting
- A threshold on a doorway

Q: The noise reduction rating (NRR) on hearing protectors:
+ Indicates how much noise the protector can reduce under lab conditions
- Is the product's price
- Shows how many hours to wear it
- Is a radio frequency

Q: Foam earplugs work best when:
+ Rolled, inserted deep into the ear canal, and held until they expand
- Placed loosely on the outside of the ear
- Cut in half
- Reused for months without cleaning

Q: A sign of overexposure to noise is:
+ Ringing in the ears or muffled hearing after work
- Better hearing after work
- Feeling hungry
- Itchy elbows

Q: A good rule of thumb is that noise may be hazardous if:
+ You must raise your voice to talk to someone an arm's length away
- You can hear birds outside
- It sounds like music
- It is quiet
