# Ransomware and incident response.

Q: If a ransom message appears on your screen, you should first:
+ Disconnect from the network and report it to IT Security immediately, without trying to fix it yourself
- Pay the ransom with a P-card
- Restart the computer repeatedly
- Email the attackers to negotiate

Q: Ransomware usually gets in through:
+ Phishing emails, stolen credentials, or unpatched systems
- Clean desk violations
- Long lunches
- Excessive use of the stapler

Q: Backups protect against ransomware best when they are:
+ Regular, tested, and stored separately from the main network
- Kept on the same server
- Made once a decade
- Stored on a sticky note

Q: Reporting a suspected security incident quickly matters because:
+ Fast containment limits the damage
- It earns rewards points
- It can be done only once a year
- It avoids paperwork

Q: You clicked a suspicious link and entered your password. You should:
+ Report it to IT Security right away and change your password as directed
- Wait to see what happens
- Delete the email and say nothing
- Blame the sender

Q: During an incident, communications with the media should come from:
+ Corporate Communications only
- Whoever answers the phone
- The person who clicked the link
- The attackers

Q: An incident response plan defines:
+ Roles, steps, and contacts for responding to a security incident
- How to respond to compliments
- The seating chart for {EVENT}
- How to return a lost umbrella

Q: Paying a ransom:
+ Does not guarantee data recovery and may violate sanctions laws; decisions are made only by authorized leadership
- Always restores everything
- Is required by insurance
- Can be done by any employee

Q: Turning off a computer infected with ransomware can sometimes:
+ Destroy evidence that investigators need, so follow IT Security's instructions
- Remove the ransomware completely
- Encrypt the files faster
- Alert the attackers
