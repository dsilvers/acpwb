# Social engineering: vishing, smishing, QR codes, and deepfakes.

Q: A text message says your package is delayed and asks you to click a link to reschedule. This could be:
+ Smishing (SMS phishing)
- A helpful reminder you should always click
- A message from the {COMMITTEE}
- An emergency alert

Q: A QR code sticker placed over the original code on a parking meter or poster may be:
+ A "quishing" attack that sends you to a malicious site
- A software update
- A coupon
- An ACPWB promotion

Q: Before entering information on a site you reached by scanning a QR code, you should:
+ Check the web address shown in your phone's preview carefully
- Trust it, since QR codes cannot be faked
- Enter your password to test it
- Share the code with coworkers

Q: A caller with the CEO's voice urgently asks you to wire money to a new vendor today. You should:
+ Verify the request through a known channel, such as a call back to a number on file, because voices can be cloned
- Send the money, since it is the CEO's voice
- Send half now and half later
- Ask the caller to email the request and then reply to that email

Q: A deepfake is:
+ Synthetic audio, video, or images created with AI to impersonate someone
- A very deep fake plant in the lobby
- A type of password
- A fake ID for the gym

Q: "Pretexting" is when an attacker:
+ Invents a believable story to get you to share information or grant access
- Sends a text before calling
- Writes a preface to a report
- Attends {EVENT} uninvited

Q: Someone in a delivery uniform asks you to hold the secure door open because their hands are full. You should:
+ Let the door close and direct them to reception
- Hold the door, since they are clearly busy
- Hold the door if the box is heavy
- Ask what is in the box, then hold the door

Q: A caller asks for a list of employee names and titles "for a survey." You should:
+ Decline and refer them to the appropriate team; this information helps attackers
- Read them the directory
- Email them the org chart
- Ask which survey and then comply

Q: A video call participant claiming to be a senior executive keeps their camera off and asks for an urgent payment. A good verification step is:
+ Ending the call and contacting the executive through a known number or channel
- Asking them to turn on their camera, then paying
- Paying, since they joined the meeting
- Asking for their favorite color

Q: A text from "the CEO" asks you to buy gift cards for a client and send the codes. This is:
+ A common scam
- A normal executive request
- An approved procurement process
- A team-building exercise

Q: Social engineering attacks most often succeed by exploiting:
+ Urgency, authority, fear, and helpfulness
- Slow computers
- Strong passwords
- Long training courses
