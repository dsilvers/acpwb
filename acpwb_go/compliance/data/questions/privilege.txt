# Attorney-client privilege basics.
Q: The attorney-client privilege generally protects:
+ Confidential communications between a client and its lawyers made to seek or give legal advice
- Every email that copies a lawyer
- Business documents labeled "privileged"
- Conversations at lunch with a lawyer about sports

Q: Marking an email "privileged" when it contains no request for legal advice:
+ Does not make it privileged
- Makes it privileged automatically
- Is required for all emails
- Protects it forever

Q: Forwarding legal advice to people outside ACPWB:
+ Can waive the privilege
- Strengthens the privilege
- Is required for transparency
- Has no effect

Q: Copying a lawyer on a routine business email:
+ Does not by itself make the email privileged
- Makes it privileged
- Is required by policy
- Waives all privilege

Q: In a company setting, the privilege usually belongs to:
+ The company, not the individual employee
- The employee who wrote the email
- The lawyer
- The court

Q: The attorney work product doctrine protects:
+ Materials prepared in anticipation of litigation
- All company work
- Products made by lawyers
- Office supplies

Q: Discussing legal advice in a large team chat channel:
+ Risks waiving privilege; keep distribution to those who need it
- Is the best practice
- Is required
- Protects it

Q: When seeking legal advice by email, you should:
+ Address it to the lawyer and state that you are asking for legal advice
- Copy as many people as possible
- Bury the question in a long thread
- Use your personal email

Q: Facts are:
+ Not privileged just because you told them to a lawyer
- Always privileged
- Privileged after a week
- Owned by the lawyer

Q: If you are unsure whether a document is privileged, you should:
+ Ask Legal before sharing it
- Share it widely
- Delete it
- Label it privileged

Q: An internal investigation directed by Legal is often structured to:
+ Preserve privilege over the lawyers' advice and work product
- Avoid all documentation
- Be public
- Exclude lawyers
